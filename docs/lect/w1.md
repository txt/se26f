## 1. Glossary

Grouped by theme. Know every term in bold.

### 1.1 The core definition

- **Software engineering.** A process where individuals or teams
  manage the creation and maintenance of products of required quality
  to meet stakeholders' needs, given the current constraints.
  Memorize it; every project grades some part of it.
- **Stakeholder.** Anyone with a stake in the system: users, buyers,
  maintainers, regulators, parents paying tuition. Stakeholders have
  *competing* requirements (parents want to see grades; students want
  privacy). Engineering negotiates between them.
- **Required quality.** Quality is defined by the community that uses
  the software, not by the builder. "Good" is elicited, not assumed —
  hence this week's repertory-grid exercise.
- **Constraint.** The limits you build inside: people, skill, time,
  money, compute. Change the constraints and the right design changes.
- **Classic SE loop (plan ↔ test, via code).** Plans generate
  expectations; tests check them; test results tell you which parts of
  the plan to extend or cut. The feedback loop is the engineering.
- **Vibe coding.** Karpathy's term: code first, skip the planning.
  You do not skip the work — you defer it into a bigger, later,
  testing bill. Q1 of this course: does vibe code == pain maintain?

### 1.2 Working with LLMs

- **Hallucination.** LLMs are stochastic; they produce *plausible*
  text, which is not the same as *true* text. ~52% of chatbot answers
  to programming questions contain incorrect information. The two NY
  lawyers who filed six invented court cases are the canonical story.
- **The prompt pattern.** Four parts, every time: (1) a role;
  (2) real evidence pasted in; (3) a fixed output format;
  (4) a ban on guessing ("if unsure, write UNKNOWN; cite file and
  line"). Format contracts make output checkable.
- **Zero-shot vs careful prompting.** Zero-shot: ask cold, get
  generic mush — confident, wrong in quiet ways. Careful: format
  first with a worked example, then real evidence, then demand
  checkable output. Same model, same rough cost, very different value.
- **Context window.** The model only knows what is in front of it.
  Paste beats reference; but big pastes cost money and push out other
  context, so paste what matters, not everything.
- **The librarian pattern.** On a large inherited repo, use the LLM
  to fetch — where things are, what matters, what to read next —
  then read that yourself. The librarian fetches; you judge.
- **LLM wiki.** Karpathy's practice: a knowledge base the model
  maintains. Raw sources stay immutable; the model owns the wiki;
  compile once instead of retrieving forever; link everything; lint
  for contradictions; start small.
- **Compact AI.** Small can beat big: only ~5% of SE-LLM papers even
  test a simpler alternative; when tested, surprises follow (SVM +
  TF-IDF beat "Big AI" on effort estimation, 100× faster). When the
  LLM bill grows, ask: is there a smaller way?

### 1.3 Eliciting "good": repertory grids

- **Repertory grid.** George Kelly's 1950s method for eliciting how
  people see a domain, without the interviewer's bias: elements are
  scored on constructs, then both are clustered.
- **Element.** A thing being judged. This week: last semester's
  project posters.
- **Construct.** A dimension with two named ends ("1 = sells a
  problem ... 5 = lists features"). Constructs come from the judges,
  not the facilitator. Vague ones die on contact with scoring.
- **Triad question.** The elicitation engine, asked again and again:
  which one of these three is different from the other two — and on
  what dimension?

## 2. Said in class, not in the notes

Conceptual material from the Aug 19 lecture with no section in
[n01](n01.md). This is examinable.

### Better, faster, cheaper: pick any two

The oldest constraint triangle. Go faster and more breaks; make it
better and it costs more; make it cheaper and something else gives.
Every project decision in this course is a pick-two decision, whether
you admit it or not.

### The V diagram, with numbers

The pre-LLM rule of thumb: one third of effort in planning, one sixth
in coding, one half in testing. The V shape matters because each
planning step generates the expectations that the matching test step
checks. Skip the left leg and the right leg has nothing to check
against — which is the vibe-coding diagram from n01, restated with
effort numbers attached.

### The Ironies of Automation (Bainbridge, 1983)

Written forty years before LLMs; reads like this morning's news. Two
ironies. First: automate the easy tasks and humans are left with only
the hard ones — for which they now have less practice. Second:
automation turns people into monitors, and people are bad monitors —
sustained vigilance collapses after roughly 30 minutes. Modern
corollary from a 2026 Atlantic story: an engineer in his own
autonomous car grew complacent; accidents take 3–4 seconds, coming
out of complacency takes ~9. If your workflow is "watch the agent
work," you are the weak component.

### Where do senior engineers come from?

AI firms say agents can replace junior engineers but not senior ones
— seniors hold the architecture and the judgment. But seniors are
grown from juniors, inside companies, over years. Fire the juniors
and in ten years there is no one who can look at a design and say
"that's not a good idea." Your career plan: become the person whose
judgment cannot be automated, which requires touching the code now.

### One line of code (three true stories)

- A PhD student used the training-set label where the test-set label
  belonged: one line in thousands. Found by outside researchers after
  the paper was public. Two reputations spent a week on the line, and
  the correction was delivered on stage in front of 230 people.
- NASA (Robin Lutz): 40% of errors in a Saturn-probe downlink pipeline
  traced to a ground-based Microsoft Access database in Ohio. Miss a
  packet from Saturn and that data is gone forever. The fragile part
  was the part nobody thought to check.
- The space shuttle reused a GPS unit proven in 50 years of
  airliners. The shuttle flies upside down, higher — it sees 13
  satellites, not 12, and the unit crashed repeatedly, including
  during reentry blackout when the shuttle must know its own position.
  Reuse is not free; context changes the component.

Moral for this course: LLMs happily introduce one-line errors, and
one-line errors can matter enormously. (Also told in class: an LLM
"cleanup" pass quietly deleted a real innovation inside 7000 lines,
unnoticed for a month.) Work in the smallest increments you can, with
every test you can.

### The engineering question

"Any fool can do it for a dollar. Can you do it for a penny?" LLMs
can invert a matrix — slowly, expensively, sometimes wrongly — while
a solved algorithm does it instantly. The likely future: lightweight
LLM dialog at the interface, fast symbolic methods under the hood.
The engineer's job is the mix-and-match. This is the compact-AI point
from n01, stated as a habit: before scaling up, price the smaller way.

### Verdicts: prompts you can check

Industry practice (via Red Hat): every prompt ships with a
*verdict* — an observable outcome that tells you whether it worked.
"No matter what happens, this run must update these files." Then a
long agent loop can be audited fast: no updated files, kill it. A
verdict is the prompt pattern's "checkable output" made operational,
and it previews M0: claims need instruments.

### The review vocabulary: SSOT and SoC

Many working engineers now spend more time reviewing than writing,
and reviews compress into shorthand:

- **SSOT — single source of truth.** Every fact lives in one place.
  Magic parameters defined twice is a review comment: "put these in
  one YAML file."
- **SoC — separation of concerns.** Keep data, model, and dialog
  apart. A database is the classic example: all of storage theory
  behind one boundary, with SQL as the ambassador language across it.
  A package that print()s to the screen violates SoC.

If AI writes more of the code, humans become the review engine —
and these words are the review engine's instruction set.

## 3. Review questions

Minute-exam format: one question, one minute, a few sentences or a
small table. Part A is definitional (low Bloom: remember, define).
Part B is synthesis (high Bloom: combine, design, judge). Worked
answers in section 4 — attempt first, then check.

### Part A: definitions

- **A1.** The course definition of software engineering rests on five
  phrases: *manage*; *creation and maintenance*; *required quality*;
  *stakeholders*; *constraints*. Define each in a line.
- **A2.** What is vibe coding, and which arrow of the classic SE loop
  does it delete? What happens to the deleted work?
- **A3.** Define hallucination. Why is "the model lied" the wrong
  description?
- **A4.** What is AI slop, and why is it a tragedy of the commons?
- **A5.** Name the four parts of the standard prompt pattern.
- **A6.** In a repertory grid, define *element*, *construct*, and the
  *triad question*.
- **A7.** What is a *verdict* in a prompt, and what problem does it
  solve?
- **A8.** Expand SSOT and SoC and give a one-line review comment
  using each.
- **A9.** Give the rule-of-thumb effort split across planning,
  coding, and testing, and say why the diagram is V-shaped.

### Part B: synthesis

- **B1.** Use both Ironies of Automation (1983) to predict two
  specific failure modes of a four-person team that adopts an
  always-on coding agent. Propose one process guard per failure mode.
- **B2.** Your team vibe-coded a working prototype over a weekend.
  Using the plan↔test feedback loop, say where the deferred cost will
  surface, and design the cheapest experiment that exposes it before
  a customer does.
- **B3.** ~52% of LLM programming answers contain errors, and humans
  miss ~39% of AI errors. Multiply those facts into an argument for
  why "generate, then eyeball" fails, then design a three-layer
  defense using this week's ideas.
- **B4.** Apply the prokaryote/eukaryote argument to your Project 1a
  fork: name two "membranes" you will build, and connect each to a
  real incident from this week (left-pad, expired domains, or the
  pager attack).
- **B5.** "Any fool can do it for a dollar; can you do it for a
  penny?" Your LLM pipeline works but the bill is growing. Give a
  principled procedure for deciding which parts stay LLM and which
  move to simpler code, citing the compact-AI evidence.

## 4. Worked answers

Brief sketches of full-mark answers. Yours may differ and still score
— markers look for the concepts, not the wording.

### Part A

- **A1.** *Manage*: someone steers — plans, priorities, feedback —
  it does not just happen. *Creation and maintenance*: the job is
  both building and keeping alive; most effort is the second.
  *Required quality*: quality defined by the using community, not
  the builder. *Stakeholders*: everyone with a stake, holding
  competing requirements that must be negotiated. *Constraints*:
  the limits built inside — people, skill, time, money, compute.
- **A2.** Coding first, planning skipped. It deletes the plan→test
  arrow: plans generate expectations and tests check them, so with no
  plan there is nothing to test against. The work is not skipped but
  deferred into a larger, later testing bill, paid after delivery.
- **A3.** Confident output that is plausible but false — invented
  facts, citations, or code. "Lied" is wrong because the model has no
  truth model at all: it always produces plausible text; sometimes
  plausible coincides with true. Defense: demand citations, check
  them.
- **A4.** Mass-produced, low-quality AI content. Commons: the
  generator captures the speed gain while reviewers and maintainers
  pay the checking cost — private benefit, socialized burden (curl's
  bug bounty died this way).
- **A5.** A role; real evidence pasted in; a fixed output format; a
  ban on guessing (UNKNOWN, cite file and line).
- **A6.** Element: a thing being judged (a poster). Construct: a
  judgment dimension with two named poles, scored 1–5. Triad
  question: "which of these three differs from the other two, and on
  what dimension?" — it forces constructs out of the judge.
- **A7.** An observable outcome written into the prompt ("this run
  must update these files"). It makes an agent's work auditable at a
  glance, so bad runs are killed fast instead of monitored — a
  workaround for humans being poor monitors.
- **A8.** Single source of truth: "these parameters appear in two
  places — define them once in a YAML file." Separation of concerns:
  "this library print()s directly — route output through the logging
  layer; keep data, model, and dialog apart."
- **A9.** One third planning, one sixth coding, one half testing.
  V-shaped because each planning level generates the expectations
  that the matching testing level checks — the legs meet at code and
  feed back.

### Part B

- **B1.** Irony 1 (deskilling): easy tasks go to the agent, so when
  the hard bug arrives no human has recent practice. Guard: rotate a
  weekly "no-agent" duty — one member fixes something by hand and
  presents it. Irony 2 (monitoring): nobody stays vigilant past ~30
  minutes of watching an agent. Guard: verdicts on every agent run
  plus CI gates, so correctness is checked by machinery, not by
  sustained attention.
- **B2.** The cost surfaces in testing and maintenance: with no plan
  there are no expectations, so bugs arrive haphazardly, after
  delivery, found by users. Cheapest experiment: write use cases for
  what the prototype supposedly does (a plan, reverse-engineered),
  then test the prototype against them. Every gap found is deferred
  planning debt made visible — for the cost of an afternoon.
- **B3.** Roughly half of generated answers carry errors and humans
  miss ~39% of those, so eyeballing alone ships errors at a rate near
  0.5 × 0.4 ≈ 20% — far too high. Defense in depth: (1) prompt layer
  — the four-part pattern with a ban on guessing and required
  citations; (2) verdict layer — observable outcomes and tests that
  must pass in CI; (3) human layer — review the diff, not the prose,
  and own it ("it's my code"), with live demos making slop expensive.
- **B4.** Membrane 1: pin and vendor dependencies (lockfiles, a local
  mirror) so an upstream deletion or expired-domain takeover cannot
  reach the build — answers left-pad and the expired-domain problem.
  Membrane 2: gate everything entering main behind
  review-before-merge plus CI, including LLM-generated patches — a
  supply chain you inspect, unlike the pager assembly line nobody
  audited. (Also acceptable: an allowlist for new packages; refusing
  dependencies with single anonymous maintainers.)
- **B5.** For each pipeline stage, record task type, accuracy, and
  cost per run. Where the task is deterministic (math, sorting,
  matrix inversion), replace with the direct algorithm — the LLM adds
  cost and error there. Where it is judgment over text, try the small
  rival first: compact-AI evidence says only ~5% of papers even test
  simpler baselines, and when tested they often win (SVM+TF-IDF, 100×
  faster and more accurate). Keep the LLM only where a measured
  baseline shows it earns its price; re-price when the subsidy ends.
