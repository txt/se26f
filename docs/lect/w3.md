## 1. Glossary

### 1.1 Kinds of requirement

- **Functional (FR)** — what it does. A use case in costume.
- **Non-functional (NFR)** — how well. Usually an *-ility*.
- **Constraint** — imposed from outside; not negotiable.
- **NFR conflict** — the -ilities fight. Security hurts usability;
  availability fights consistency. Choose, and write the choice
  down. Explicit trade-offs read as engineering; silent ones read
  as luck.

### 1.2 Requirement quality

- **IEEE 29148** — clear, correct, consistent, complete, feasible,
  testable, traceable.
- **Testable** — has a number and an oracle. "Must be fast" is a
  mood. "95% of searches under 2s at 10k users" is a requirement.
- **Traceable** — walks to a test, a commit, a stakeholder.
  Requirements in versioned markdown, not Word.
- **No oracle, no requirement.**
- **Requirements drift** — nobody deletes the doc, they just stop
  reading it. If your use cases have not changed in three weeks of
  coding, you are drifting.
- **V-diagram** — requirements top left, acceptance tests top
  right. Same statement in two moods.

### 1.3 Failure catalogue

Never a compiler error. The same dozen mistakes recur.

**Classes of requirements error**

- **E1 Omission** — a needed statement never written.
- **E2 Ambiguity** — one sentence, two readings.
- **E3 Imprecision** — right shape, wrong detail.
- **E4 Stale assumption under reuse** — true in the old context.
- **E5 Orphaned requirement** — a design change removes what
  satisfied it; it transfers, unwritten.
- **E6 Interface gap** — both sides complete, the seam unowned.
- **E7 Excluded stakeholder** — those who knew were not asked, or
  were asked and overridden.
- **E8 Silent priority override** — a conflict resolved with no
  decision recorded.
- **E9 Dropped verification** — the check was specified, then
  skipped as redundant.
- **E10 Misjudged criticality** — ruled out of the critical path,
  and in it.
- **E11 Unexercised envelope** — never run at real load, with real
  users, at real speed.
- **E12 No end-to-end owner** — every part specified, the whole
  nobody's.

**The cases**

- **Ariane 5, 1996** — Ariane 4 inertial code, steeper
  trajectory, 16-bit overflow; identical backup failed
  identically. → **E4**, **E1**, and **E4** again in the
  redundancy design.
- **Mariner 1, 1962** — a missing overbar. → **E3**, pure.
- **SOHO, 1998** *(lecture)* — three recovery conditions read as
  OR not AND; lost sun lock, froze, woke months later. → **E2**;
  also **E11**, never exercised.
- **London Ambulance, 1992** — gathered from managers against
  dispatcher advice. → **E7**, **E11**.
- **Mars Climate Orbiter, 1999** — pound-force vs newton-seconds.
  → **E6**, **E1**, **E12**. Process failure, not type system.
- **Denver 1995 / Heathrow T5 2008** — staff could not log in or
  park; bags flooded. → **E11**, **E7**. Same two classes
  thirteen years apart.
- **Therac-25, 1985-87** — hardware interlocks removed, software
  trusted, race on fast typing. → **E5**, **E4**.
- **Genesis 2004 / Stardust 2006** — accelerometers backwards;
  centrifuge test skipped as redundant given heritage. → **E9**,
  **E4**. Stardust honoured it and landed clean: same design, one
  class apart.
- **737 MAX** — no-retraining constraint, MCAS out of the
  manuals. → **E8**, **E7**.
- **Healthcare.gov, 2013** — nobody owned "a citizen can enrol";
  six enrolled day one. → **E12**, **E11**.
- **NHS NPfIT** — imposed on hospitals that said it would not
  fit. → **E7**, **E12**.
- **Carrier deck / McCain** *(lecture)* — each deck dropped a
  check assuming the other held it. → **E5** twice, **E6**. No
  software, which is the point.
- **Gulf War friendly fire** *(lecture)* — a ground logging
  database nobody classed as flight-critical. → **E10**.
- **Reuse means more testing** *(lecture)* — at NASA, heavy reuse
  got a *larger* test team. → The counterweight to **E4**.

**Carry into 1b.** E4, E7 and E11 cover most of the table and are
cheap to check: what did we reuse and on what assumptions, who
did we not ask, what have we never run for real.

### 1.4 Constraints: law, standards, licences

**Classes of legal issue**

- **L1 Duty attaching to data** — privacy; triggered by storing.
- **L2 Duty attaching to conduct** — what it may do, emit, claim.
- **L3 Circumvention as fraud** — gaming the test is worse than
  failing it.
- **L4 Accessibility duty** — civil-rights statute reaching
  software, with a technical yardstick.
- **L5 Standard as negligence baseline** — a published baseline
  becomes the bar after an incident.
- **L6 Inbound licence obligation** — terms of code you consume.
- **L7 Outbound licence choice** — what you grant, and its
  compatibility with L6.
- **L8 IP scope of interfaces** — is it ownable at all.
- **L9 Jurisdictional reach** — the law follows the user.
- **L10 Personal liability** — the engineer, not just the firm.
- **L11 Human-subject duties** — consent, anonymity, refusal,
  erasure, sunset.

**The cases**

- **Volkswagen** — code that detected the rig. >$30bn, criminal
  convictions, a manager in federal prison. → **L2**, **L3**,
  **L10**, **L9**.
- **GDPR** — Meta paid €1.2B in 2023. → **L1**, **L9**. Trigger
  is storage, not sale; most student projects are in scope and
  assume they are not.
- **Domino's / Robles** — fought a blind customer's ADA claim to
  the Supreme Court rather than fix the site. → **L4**, with WCAG
  as the **L5** yardstick.
- **Equifax, 2017** — unpatched Struts CVE, 147M records. →
  **L5**, **L1**.
- **GPL** — free like a puppy is free; Cisco and Vizio sued into
  compliance. → **L6** colliding with **L7**. An LLM will paste
  GPL into your MIT repo and say nothing.
- **Copyleft as inversion** *(lecture)* — Stallman used the
  instrument of lock-up to force openness. → **L7** as strategy,
  not boilerplate.
- **Oracle v. Google** — $9bn, eleven years, fair use 2021. →
  **L8**. The other way, nobody reimplements an interface.
- **Human subjects** *(lecture)* — Facebook A/B tests you without
  asking. Anonymity with a sunset, refusal, erasure. → **L11**,
  **L1**. Logging behaviour to tune yourself is experimenting on
  users.

**Constraint-mining prompt.** Paste stakeholders and sketch:
"which regulations, standards and licences constrain this; cite
the clause; write UNKNOWN if unsure." Then verify every citation.

### 1.5 Stakeholders

- **Stakeholder** — anyone who affects or is affected by the
  system. The starter list is always too short.
- **Six groups** — users; clients/sponsors; domain experts;
  developers/maintainers; regulators/legal; indirect and negative.
- **Indirect/negative** — the courier whose pay algorithm you set,
  the street your scooters park in. Matters most, found last.
- **Power-interest grid** — high/high manage closely; high
  power low interest keep satisfied; low power high interest keep
  informed; low/low monitor.
- **The bottom-left box** — courier, dispatcher, patient, blind
  customer. The grid says ignore them; the war stories say what
  that costs. Ethics lives there.
- **Who decides** — if nobody decides on purpose, the org chart
  decides by accident.
- **Stakeholder as revenue** *(lecture)* — the restaurant chain
  as stakeholder yields near-expiry clearance, promotion echo, a
  paid tier. Couriers as stakeholders become road sensors.

### 1.6 Elicitation

- **Elicitation** — extracting requirements from people who
  cannot state them.
- **Four reasons asking fails** — people do not know; people lie
  politely; watching changes behaviour; the loudest voice wins.
- **The workarounds ARE the requirements.**
- **Multiple instruments** — interviews, observation, CRC cards,
  repertory grids, LLM role-play. Never push a blank sheet across
  the table.
- **Repertory grid** — three things they know, which two are
  alike and how does the third differ. Their constructs, not
  yours. Cluster it; constructs that travel together are one
  value wearing two labels.
- **Grid economics** *(lecture)* — an hour per stakeholder, five
  a week, over lunch. Often reveals you were asked for one
  product and there are three, one of which makes the rest cheap.
- **LLM as partner** — "You are the courier. What do you fear?"
  Biased, synthetic, still finds what the team missed.
- **Taboo-search prompting** *(lecture)* — ten rivals, grid the
  differences, then "now tell me what nobody is doing."

## 2. Beyond the notes

### 2.1 Elicitation: the cognitive theory underneath

The notes say people do not know what they want. The lecture said
why.

**Two-memory model.** A tiny short-term memory holding features
extracted from the world; a vast long-term memory of
condition-action rules. STM matches LTM, rules fire, STM is
rewritten, round again.

**What expertise is.** Not seeing more. Having rules that decide
what to put into STM and what to ignore. A chess player sees "my
knight forks his king and queen," not a board of pixels.

**Why the expert cannot tell you.** LTM is acquired at roughly
ten rules a day over years, and those rules are compiled past
readability with no introspective access. So humans cannot report
their own expertise. Ask a driver to write down how they drive
and you will not get "watch the car opposite even with its
indicator on, because everyone lies with their indicator."

**Consequences.**

- A blank sheet harvests the reportable tenth.
- This is not politeness or performance. Those are motivational.
  This one is architectural: the stakeholder is cooperating fully
  and still cannot answer.
- So observe behaviour, and use methods that ask people to
  *discriminate* rather than *describe*. Discrimination runs on
  the compiled rules directly. That is why grids work.
- Ford's horse and Jobs' iPod are the same point at product
  scale: people judge what is in front of them far better than
  they specify what does not exist.

**The analyst gap.** Business users know the view, not the
structure. You hear "trucks, routes, San Diego" and are already
building nodes and arcs; they will never say directed graph, or
normalised. They are not vague — that surface is the only one
they have. Feeding your vocabulary back gets you agreement to a
design nobody understood.

### 2.2 CRC cards, fully worked

Class - Responsibilities - Collaborators. One component per card.

**The physical protocol, and why it is physical.**

- Three-by-five index cards. **Pencils, not pens.**
- Done properly the floor ends up littered with torn-up cards.
  That is the success condition, not a mess.
- Cheapness is the design. A card costs nothing to destroy, so
  nobody defends a bad structure to protect their own effort.
  Compare reversing a diagram someone spent an afternoon on.
- Whole team, standing up, an hour.

```
+-----------------------------------+
| ClassName                         |
+-----------------+-----------------+
| Responsibilities| Collaborators   |
| - short phrase  | - OtherClass    |
| - short phrase  | - OtherClass    |
+-----------------+-----------------+
```

**Running the bar scenario.** Hand out roles as objects:
bartender, glass, till, stool, cash register. Someone plays the
customer and walks a story through, out loud.

- "Bartender, pour me a drink." Bartender's responsibility: take
  money, return a full glass.
- The bar has beer glasses, whisky glasses, and Guinness.
  Guinness needs a two-part pour and a settle; whisky is one
  shot.
- **Who knows how to pour?** The instinct is the bartender. But
  the *glass* should know: "Whisky glass, fill yourself." Now the
  bartender never touches the taps, and a new drink adds a card
  instead of editing one.
- Push on. Should the bartender take money? Maybe. Do the
  accounting and reordering? Probably not — and after a few
  passes you find the **till**, which already holds opening
  inventory and sees everything leave. So inventory lives there,
  and therefore so does reordering. Fewer messages, one owner.

Nobody designed that. The story walked it out. That is the
output of a CRC session.

**Smells the session makes visible.**

- **God class** — one card carries everything: a bartender who
  pours, charges, tallies and reorders.
- **Dead object** — a card with no responsibilities: the glass,
  the cash register, if the bartender kept it all.
- God classes and dead objects are the same smell from two ends.
  The fix is to push responsibilities off the fat card onto the
  dead ones — buying a simpler thing to build and to test.
- **Long message chain** — one event ricochets across the whole
  board. Layer it so a developer touches a few hops.

**Sizing heuristics.** 3-4 responsibilities per class, 5-6
methods per responsibility. So ~20 methods, 40 at the outside.
More than that, it belongs elsewhere.

**Scaling up.** Cards on a board group into subsystem bubbles —
finance here, security there (bouncer, cameras), health
management above. The bubbles are your architecture, discovered
rather than declared.

**Why it is in a requirements lecture.** CRC is elicitation, not
design. The business user can play the bartender. They cannot
read your class diagram. Running the story extracts the compiled
rules from 2.1 without asking anyone to describe them.

## 3. Review questions

Ten questions, ~7 minutes each. **(a)** recall, **(b)** analysis
or transfer.

**1. Kinds of requirement**
(a) Define FR, NFR and constraint with one food-delivery example
each.
(b) Argue the boundary is about who can change the requirement
rather than what it says. Where does that framing break?

**2. Testability**
(a) Why is "the system must be secure" not a requirement?
Rewrite it as a testable NFR.
(b) Maintainability resists numbers. Make it testable without
inventing a metric that measures the wrong thing.

**3. Reuse (E4)**
(a) State the requirements-level cause of Ariane 5 in one
sentence.
(b) At NASA, heavy reuse got a *larger* test team. Square that
with reuse-saves-effort, and apply the result to pasting
LLM-generated code into your repo.

**4. Interfaces and ambiguity (E6, E2)**
(a) What was unwritten in Mars Climate Orbiter, and what was
misread in SOHO?
(b) Tim called MCO a process failure and rejected "use a stronger
type system." Make his case, then the strongest case against it.

**5. Orphaned requirements (E5)**
(a) State the Therac-25 lesson about removing a safety layer, and
say how the carrier-deck story is the same class.
(b) Name one property of your 1b product currently guaranteed
outside your code — framework, platform, manual step. What
happens when that guarantee goes, and who notices?

**6. Dropped verification (E9)**
(a) Why did Genesis crash when Stardust did not?
(b) "Heritage" justified the skip. Write the rule for when a
heritage argument is legitimate and when it is an excuse.

**7. Stakeholders**
(a) List the six groups with one example each, and give the
action for each cell of the power-interest grid.
(b) Name the highest-interest, lowest-power stakeholder of your
1b product. What would change if they had a veto, and why have
you not changed it anyway?

**8. Silent override (E8) and ownership (E12)**
(a) In the 737 MAX, which stakeholder outranked which? What
failed at Healthcare.gov given the parts mostly worked?
(b) "If nobody decides on purpose, the org chart decides by
accident." Apply it to a decision your team made without
discussing it.

**9. Why asking fails**
(a) List the four reasons, and sketch the two-memory model.
(b) Three reasons are motivational, one is architectural.
Identify which, and explain why the fix cannot be "ask more
carefully." Then state a condition under which grids fail too.

**10. CRC cards**
(a) What goes on a card, why pencils, and what are the sizing
heuristics?
(b) Argue for putting pour logic on the glass, then on the
bartender. What decides it — and is the answer the same for the
till and reordering?
