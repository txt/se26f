# N10: Maintenance — Living With Other People's Code (Oct 21)

Tonight proj2 dies and proj3 is born: you inherit another team's
proj2, and another team inherits yours. Everything in this lecture is
about the month you are about to have. The exam-night proj3 preview
(Oct 14) gave you the economics — most of the life of software is
maintenance, and maintainers spend most of *their* life just reading.
Tonight is the practice: how to understand code you did not write,
how to see the debt hiding in it, how to change it without breaking
it, and exactly what to do in your first week with the inherited
repo.

Rough shape of the 90 minutes: the COBOL story; what maintenance
actually is; comprehension and code archaeology; technical debt, with
configuration as the worked, measured example; migration and
modernization, with lessons from people who did it for real;
refactoring under test; then the proj3 handover mechanics.

---

## 1. The COBOL story: the language that refused to die

### 1.1 The numbers, because they are absurd

- Estimates run to **hundreds of billions of lines of COBOL** still
  in production — supporting around **$3 trillion in daily
  commerce**. One veteran's line: "the second most valuable asset in
  the United States — after oil — is the 240 billion lines of COBOL."
- When a credit or debit card is swiped, roughly **95% of the time a
  COBOL program runs** somewhere behind it. Around 43% of US core
  banking systems are COBOL. By transaction count, COBOL systems
  process **orders of magnitude more transactions daily than Google
  serves searches**.
- The language shipped in **1959**, designed in committee as a
  stop-gap, with Grace Hopper's fingerprints on it. Its two design
  bets: English-like readability over brevity (`MOVE X TO Y`), and
  portability — it has run on hundreds of platforms. Both bets were
  about *other people later*, which is why it is tonight's opening
  act: COBOL is the longest-running maintenance experiment in
  history.
- The average COBOL programmer is somewhere between 45 and 55, and
  scarce enough that salaries carry a visible premium. Retirement is
  the clock on the whole story.

### 1.2 So replace it. People have tried.

- **Commonwealth Bank of Australia** replaced its core banking
  platform: five years, over AU$1 billion — about double the budget,
  roughly two years late. That is the *success* story.
- **TSB Bank, 2018**: migrated a billion customer records onto a new
  system. Customers locked out for weeks; some saw *other people's
  accounts*; roughly £330M in migration costs plus £49M lost to
  fraud during the meltdown, and the CEO lost his job.
- **New York Times** rewrote its COBOL circulation system in Java —
  successfully — and still blew the schedule, because the hard part
  was proving the new system replicated *every* behavior of the old
  one. Nobody had the old behavior written down. The old system was
  its own only spec.
- Zoom out: the US federal government was spending about **$90
  billion a year — roughly 80% of its IT budget — maintaining legacy
  systems**. And the Y2K remediation, the biggest scheduled
  maintenance event ever, cost around $320 billion worldwide — most
  of it *reading* code to find two-digit years.

### 1.3 And when you do not replace it, it bites anyway

In 2020, COVID unemployment claims surged 1,600% in New Jersey and
the governor went on television asking for volunteer COBOL
programmers. A dozen states were in the same hole. Congress shaped
the CARES Act around a flat $600 boost partly because states said
changing a *percentage* in their unemployment systems would take five
months. Meanwhile a 75-year-old Texan runs a company called COBOL
Cowboys ("Not our first rodeo") renting out ~350 retired engineers
for exactly these emergencies. His quote to Reuters: "Some of the
software I wrote for banks in the 1970s is still being used."

### 1.4 Why it survives (it is not nostalgia)

- **It is genuinely good at its job.** Fixed-format records, indexed
  file access, and native decimal arithmetic make it brutally fast at
  batch transaction processing — the exact workload banks have.
  (Binary floating point rounds money wrong; COBOL's decimal types
  were designed by people who had met an auditor.)
- **The hidden economics.** The original systems replaced thousands
  of humans doing manual data entry — a stupendous return on
  investment. Replacing one working computer system with another
  computer system returns almost nothing, at enormous risk. First
  automation pays; re-automation is a rounding error with a tail
  risk.
- **The paradox stated straight**: a 2024 survey found 92% of
  organizations call their COBOL "strategic," and half expect to be
  running *more* of it next year. The language is not legacy because
  it is old; it is legacy because everything depends on it.

### 1.5 Three lessons, and they are the lecture

1. **Code outlives everything** — its authors, its language's
   fashion, sometimes its company. Software is the only engineering
   artifact we routinely run for 50 years while pretending it is
   temporary.
2. **The code is not the expensive part; the behavior is.** The NYT
   project was hard because the old system's behavior was the real
   spec. (You met this in testing as the oracle problem — here it is
   at the scale of a whole system.)
3. **The maintenance bill is mostly a comprehension bill.** Nobody
   pays COBOL Cowboys to type. They pay them to *understand*.

And for this course's Q1: COBOL systems were not vibe-coded. They
came from the most planning-heavy era in software history, with
operations manuals and test decks — and they are *still* this
expensive to maintain. Now price the maintenance of code that was
generated fast, reviewed lightly, and never really understood by
anyone. You will find out personally, starting tonight.

## 2. What maintenance actually is

### 2.1 Four kinds (ISO's taxonomy, worth memorizing)

- **Corrective** — fixing faults that users found. The one everybody
  pictures; historically only ~20% of the effort.
- **Adaptive** — the world moved: new OS, new API version, new
  regulation, new tax year. The system did not break; its environment
  did.
- **Perfective** — users liked it, so they want more: new features,
  better performance on the paths they actually use. Historically the
  *biggest* share, around half.
- **Preventive** — repaying debt before it bites: refactoring,
  re-documenting, retiring dead knobs. The share that gets cut first
  and missed most.

The surprise in that list: **maintenance is mostly not bug-fixing.**
It is mostly the system succeeding — being used enough that people
demand it keep up with a moving world. Which reframes proj3: you are
not inheriting a crime scene, you are inheriting something that
worked well enough to survive to handover, plus the bill for that
survival.

### 2.2 Lehman's laws (1970s, measured on IBM OS/360, still true)

Manny Lehman watched large systems evolve release by release and
wrote down what always happened. Four of his laws earn their keep
here:

1. **Continuing change** — a system that is used *must* change,
   because its environment changes. Freeze it and it becomes
   progressively less useful (ask the states running 40-year-old
   unemployment systems).
2. **Increasing complexity** — as it changes, structure decays
   *unless* effort is spent specifically to hold structure. Entropy
   is the default; preventive maintenance is the counter-spend.
3. **Conservation of familiarity** — each release can only absorb so
   much change before the people around it (developers, users) lose
   the plot. Big-bang releases violate this law and the law wins;
   this is also why TSB hurt so much.
4. **Continuing growth** — functionality only ever accretes, because
   removing a feature angers someone who used it. (Hold this thought;
   it returns as the knobs data in §4, measured.)

Maintenance, in Lehman's framing, is not keeping the system the same.
It is running to stay in place.

### 2.3 Where the maintainer's day goes

The folklore numbers — and your proj3 experience will roughly match —
are that maintainers spend about half their time just *reading*:
code, histories, issues, docs that lie. Writing the fix is the small
end of the funnel. That is why the next section is the longest
skill-section of the lecture, and why "I'm a fast coder" is not the
proj3 skill. "I'm a fast *understander*" is.

## 3. Comprehension at scale

### 3.1 Why understanding is the bottleneck

**Knowledge decays through handoffs.** From the
[patterns recap](w6.md): each handoff of a codebase roughly halves
the understanding — 100% in the author's head, 50% in the first
maintainer's, 25% in the next. Proj3 is handoff one. The team that
wrote your inherited repo knew why that weird branch exists; you do
not, and by November neither will they.

**Experts read in chunks.** The chess story from
[the patterns lecture](n06.md) is really a story about reading code:
the beginner sees lines, the expert sees "that's a factory," "that's
a retry wrapper," "that's a God class." Every pattern name you
learned is a unit of reading speed. Comprehension at scale is mostly
having chunks — which is why pattern vocabulary sits in a
maintenance course at all.

**Your own code counts as other people's code.** From
[the patterns lecture](n06.md): "your own code three months later is
always incomprehensible." Three months is roughly the gap between
your proj2 kickoff and proj3's end. Every comment you wrote for the
inheriting team, you also wrote for December-you.

### 3.2 Code archaeology: the history is documentation that cannot lie

The repo's git history is the one account of the system nobody could
forget to update. Mine it before you trust any prose:

- **`git log --follow` on a confusing file** — the commit messages
  are the missing comments, and the dates tell you which decisions
  are load-bearing antiques.
- **`git log -S someName`** — "when did this identifier appear or
  vanish?" finds the commit where a behavior was born, faster than
  any grep of the present.
- **Churn** — files touched by many commits and many authors are
  where the design is contested; they are also, per **bug locality**
  ([patterns recap](w6.md): "bugs are social animals"), where the
  next bug statistically lives. `git log` piped through `sort | uniq
  -c | sort -n` is a defect predictor you can build in one line —
  and note that is the same three-signal trick (new? failed before?
  how long since exercised?) that fixed Google's two-week test
  latency.
- **Blame, used kindly** — `git blame` answers "who can I ask," not
  "who do I shame." On an inherited repo it answers "which commit
  explains this," which is usually enough.

### 3.3 Reading order for an alien repo

A repeatable recipe, cheapest signal first:

1. **README's claims vs reality** — run what it says to run; the gap
   is your first measurement.
2. **The tests** — the only description of the system that goes red
   when it drifts. Read the test *names* as a table of contents for
   intended behavior. No tests? That finding rewrites your whole
   month (§6).
3. **The entry points** — main, the route table, the CLI parser:
   the complete list of ways the outside world gets in.
4. **The data** — schemas, file formats, the shapes passed between
   layers. From [the architecture lecture](n04.md): the model layer
   holds what is true, so whoever understands the data model owns
   the quickest map of the system.
5. **One trace, end to end** — pick the most important user action
   and follow it through every layer, taking notes. One deep trace
   beats ten shallow skims.
6. **Scratch refactoring** (Feathers' trick): rename, extract, and
   simplify *just to force yourself to understand* — then **throw
   the branch away**. The learning was the point; keeping
   uninsured edits is not.

### 3.4 The librarian, again

From [the survival kit](w1.md): on a large inherited repo, use the
LLM to *fetch* — where things are, what matters, what to read next —
then read that yourself. The librarian fetches; you judge. §6 turns
this into a concrete proj3 deliverable (regenerate the LLM wiki,
then audit it), and the audit matters because a plausible wrong map
of alien code is worse than no map: you cannot smell the error,
precisely because the code is alien.

## 4. Technical debt, properly

### 4.1 The metaphor, used precisely

Ward Cunningham's coinage. Debt is not "bad code." Debt is a *loan*:
you shipped sooner by deferring work, and you now pay **interest** —
every change costs extra because the deferred work is still missing —
until you repay the **principal** by doing that work. Some loans are
excellent engineering: ship the demo now, restructure after the
market says yes. The metaphor's point is *management*, not guilt:
debt you know about, with a plan, is a tool; debt nobody wrote down
is just rot with a nicer name.

Martin Fowler's quadrant gives the honest axes: the loan was
**deliberate or inadvertent**, and **prudent or reckless**.
"We'll ship without docs and write them next sprint" is
deliberate-prudent. "What's layering?" is reckless-inadvertent — and
an unwatched code generator produces reckless-inadvertent debt at
machine speed, which is the Q1 of this course restated in accounting
terms.

### 4.2 A field guide: five kinds you will find in the inherited repo

- **Code debt** — duplication, God classes, dead code: the whole
  smell catalogue from [the patterns lecture](n06.md). Interest paid:
  every change visits more places than it should.
- **Test debt** — missing tests, weak asserts, flaky tests, the
  suite nobody runs. Interest paid: fear. Teams with test debt stop
  changing things, and Lehman's first law punishes standing still.
- **Documentation debt** — docs that drifted from the code. Worse
  than absent docs, because they are confidently wrong (requirements
  drift, from [requirements week](w3.md), is this disease upstream).
- **Dependency debt** — pinned ancient versions, abandoned
  upstreams, licenses nobody checked. This is where **left-pad** and
  **xz** (both from earlier weeks) live: 75–95% of a modern system is
  code you downloaded, and every dependency is a maintenance contract
  you signed on someone else's behalf. Equifax, from
  [requirements week](w3.md), was dependency debt with a $700M
  interest payment.
- **Configuration debt** — every knob, flag, and setting. Measured
  in detail next, because it is the kind you can *count* in week one.

### 4.3 Self-admitted technical debt: the repo confesses

Researchers call comments like `TODO`, `FIXME`, `HACK`, `XXX`
**self-admitted technical debt (SATD)** — places where the author
*knew* they were borrowing and said so in writing. Mining studies
find these comments live for years, and cluster exactly where you
would fear. For proj3: `grep -rn "TODO\|FIXME\|HACK\|XXX"` over the
inherited repo is a sixty-second debt audit, written for you by the
very people who took the loans. Put the count, and the three
scariest, into your triage report.

### 4.4 When to repay: two disciplines

- **The boy-scout rule** (leave the campsite cleaner than you found
  it): while you are in a file anyway, make one small improvement —
  in its own commit (§5 explains why). Compounds quietly; never
  blocks a schedule.
- **Priced repayments**: for anything bigger, repay only where
  *interest is high* — code you change often, on your critical path.
  Perfectly rotten code you never touch charges no interest; leave
  it. The debt ledger in §6 exists to make exactly this call with
  numbers instead of disgust.

### 4.5 The worked example: configuration debt

The sharpest measured case of inadvertent debt. Xu et al.,
["Hey, you have given me too many knobs!"](https://doi.org/10.1145/2786805.2786852)
(FSE 2015) studied a commercial storage system (Storage-A) plus
Apache, MySQL and Hadoop:

<img src="https://raw.githubusercontent.com/txt/se26f/refs/heads/main/etc/img/knobs-growth.png" width="420" align=right>

- Parameter counts **only ever grow** (figure at right) — Lehman's
  continuing-growth law, in four frames. Parameters are added ~7×
  faster than they are removed. Hadoop MapReduce went from 17 to 173
  parameters in seven years; MySQL 5.6 had 461, over 200 of them
  with complex types.
- **Up to 54% of parameters are set by almost nobody**; only 6–17%
  are set by a majority of users. Of numeric parameters, up to 47%
  show no more than five distinct values across all users — the
  flexibility was never wanted.
- The cost is real: up to 48.5% of user configuration issues are
  people struggling to *find or set* the right knob, and roughly
  half of configuration errors come from users keeping a default
  they should have changed. Configuration error is a leading cause
  of production outages at major cloud services — it out-crashes
  code bugs in several published studies.
- Their guidelines could remove **51.9%** of Storage-A's parameters
  with little impact on real users.

<img src="https://raw.githubusercontent.com/txt/se26f/refs/heads/main/etc/img/knobs-configurable.png" width="420" align=right>

Here is the moment the debt is born (figure at right): a real
developer discussion from HDFS. *"Since we are not sure what is a
good choice, how about making it configurable?"* Searching mailing
lists, support tickets and a user survey, the researchers found
**zero users** who ever set that parameter. Every knob like this is
a decision the developer declined to make, exported to thousands of
users — each one a line of documentation, a dimension in the test
matrix (remember **all-pairs testing** from
[deeper testing week](w5.md) — that whole technique exists because
of config spaces like these), a support-ticket generator, and attack
surface.

You have met this shape twice before: the Word-ribbon audit
([patterns lecture](n06.md) — 90% of use in 10 of 60 icons), and the
Google feature-toggle spiderweb
([architecture lecture](n04.md)). Sven Apel calls the research
version the *configuration abyss* (interview in IEEE Software,
2024): the configuration space is so large that nobody — developers
included — knows which corner of it is actually running in
production. Which breaks "fly what you test" silently: with hundreds
of binary knobs there are more configurations than tests you will
ever run, so almost every fielded configuration is untested by
construction.

Rob Pike, quoted in the paper: "There is too much configuration.
There are too many options. Stuff should just work."

**Proj3 translation.** Count your inherited repo's knobs: config
files, environment variables, CLI flags, magic constants promoted to
"settings." For each ask one question — *who sets this, ever?* A
knob nobody sets is debt you can retire in week one, and retiring it
is a measurable maintenance contribution. When you do, remember the
single-source-of-truth rule: one home per surviving setting, and
delete in their own commits (§1.5 of your week-one playbook: the
coconut rule — "unused" is a claim about your knowledge, so
instrument first, delete second, revert-in-one-command always).

## 5. Migration and modernization: lessons from people who did it

### 5.1 First, the vocabulary (old words, still on exams)

- **Reverse engineering** — recovering design and intent from an
  artifact: reading, tracing, diagramming. Produces understanding,
  changes nothing.
- **Restructuring / refactoring** — same behavior, better structure,
  same technology.
- **Re-engineering** — reverse engineer, then build forward again:
  keep the business rules, re-implement the system.
- **Migration** — move to a new platform, language, or architecture,
  behavior held constant.

### 5.2 The decision: a portfolio, not a mood

So the inherited system is debt-heavy. Rewrite it? Almost never —
§1 priced that. Wesley Assunção (NC State, down the hall — his group
studies exactly this) frames the real menu as portfolio analysis:

<img src="https://raw.githubusercontent.com/txt/se26f/refs/heads/main/etc/img/modernize-quadrant.png" width="430" align=right>

**Five options by business value × technical quality** (figure at
right, from
[Contemporary Software Modernization](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.04017)):
low value, low quality → **replace** with something off the shelf;
high quality, low value → just **maintain**; high value, high
quality → **evolve** (new features on good bones); high value, low
quality → **re-engineer** (preserve the business rules, pay the
technical debt); and when adopting new technology *is* the goal →
**migrate**. Note what the grid does: it forbids the lazy question
"is this code good?" and forces the paired question "good, *and
worth what?*" Perfect code nobody needs is quadrant one; dreadful
code the business lives on is quadrant four, and quadrant four is
where re-engineering earns a salary. Your inherited proj2 sits
somewhere on this grid, and your proj3 plan should say where, with a
measurement attached.

### 5.3 Lessons from industrial migrations

From the migrations Assunção's group reports (a large-scale
industrial migration to microservices, SANER 2026; LLM-assisted
re-engineering of a legacy system at Volvo, SANER 2025) — and every
war story this course has told:

1. **Never big-bang.** Lehman's conservation-of-familiarity law,
   enforced by arithmetic: a working migration runs old and new side
   by side and moves one slice at a time. The named pattern is the
   **strangler fig**: new growth wraps the old tree, takes over one
   branch at a time, and the old trunk dies only at the end. TSB is
   what the other plan costs.
2. **You met the testing half already.** **Shadow testing**
   ([deeper testing week](w5.md)): the old system serves the user
   while the new one runs silently beside it on the same traffic,
   and you diff outputs. Its known limit applies doubly in
   migration: if the old system is wrong, matching it faithfully
   reproduces the wrong behavior — *and sometimes that is exactly
   what you want*, because downstream systems depend on the old
   bug. Decide bug-for-bug compatibility questions explicitly, in
   writing, per interface.
3. **Two structural tools for the in-between months.** An
   **anti-corruption layer**: a thin translator between old and new
   so the new code is not slowly colonized by the old system's
   vocabulary and quirks (it is the adapter pattern from
   [the patterns lecture](n06.md), doing border control). And
   **branch by abstraction**: put an interface in front of the old
   component, point everyone at the interface, then swap
   implementations behind it — Parnas's interface-first discipline
   from [the architecture lecture](n04.md), used as a migration
   vehicle. Note what both require: the seams are the migration
   plan. Architecture week is why migration month is survivable.
4. **The old system's behavior is the spec** — including behaviors
   nobody meant. Budget for *discovering* requirements, not just
   porting code. Reuse means more testing, not less — the NASA
   lesson from [requirements week](w3.md).
5. **LLMs help at the comprehension end** — summarizing, mapping,
   drafting ports — and every output needs an audit, because
   plausible wrong summaries of legacy code are landmines (Volvo's
   experience, and your §6 wiki task).
6. **Organizations migrate, not just code.** Who owns the seam while
   half the system is old and half new? If nobody, that is the
   interface-gap failure (Mars Climate Orbiter,
   [requirements week](w3.md)) scheduled months in advance.

<img src="https://raw.githubusercontent.com/txt/se26f/refs/heads/main/etc/img/modernize-workflow.png" width="90%">

Their workflow sketch (above) is initiation → planning → execution →
transition — with arrows pointing *backwards*, because you will
return to planning mid-execution, on purpose. Proj3 is this diagram
at course scale: tonight is initiation; your first week is planning;
your November is execution; your report is the transition document
for whoever reads the repo next.

## 6. Refactoring under test

### 6.1 The iron rule

Refactoring: changing structure while preserving behavior. The
definition hides a trap — "preserving behavior" is a *claim*, and
this course's standing doctrine is that claims need instruments. So:

> **No green suite, no refactoring.** A refactor without tests is
> just editing with confidence.

### 6.2 But the inherited repo is thin on tests

Michael Feathers' definition: **legacy code is code without tests** —
regardless of age. Code written last month with no suite is legacy;
COBOL with a good test deck is not. His escape route is your proj3
playbook:

1. **Characterization tests first.** Before changing anything, write
   tests that pin down what the code *currently does* — not what it
   should do. Run the function, observe the answer, assert that
   answer. You are not approving the behavior; you are fencing it so
   change becomes detectable. (This is the plan↔test loop run
   backwards — reverse-engineering expectations from an artifact,
   the same move as the vibe-coding recovery experiment in
   [week one](w1.md).) The bulk version is the **golden master**:
   capture the system's full output on a pile of representative
   inputs, store it, and diff every future run against it — a
   poor-man's shadow test you can build in an afternoon.
2. **Find the seams.** A seam (Feathers' word) is a place where you
   can change behavior without editing the code — pass in a
   different collaborator, substitute a fake behind an interface.
   No seams is why legacy code is hard to test (the design-for-test
   lesson from [the architecture lecture](n04.md), read in a
   mirror); making a seam — extract the dependency, pass it in — is
   usually the first refactoring, done under the thinnest possible
   characterization fence.
3. **Small steps, test between each.** The named moves from
   Fowler's catalogue — rename, extract function, inline, move — are
   small *on purpose*: each is near-provably behavior-preserving, so
   the suite runs between every step and the batch size of possible
   breakage is one step. The one-line-of-code stories from
   [week one](w1.md) are the reason one step is the right batch
   size. (Two cheap non-negotiables while you are in there: the
   doorman fix from [the architecture lecture](n04.md) — push
   coercion and parsing to the edges — and dead-knob deletion from
   §4.5.)
4. **Audit the net before trusting it.** A green suite that asserts
   nothing is a fence made of fog — the order-controller test from
   [the patterns recap](w6.md) passed for a whole semester *because*
   of its bug. **Mutation-test the inherited suite** before leaning
   on it: break the code on purpose; if nothing goes red, extend the
   suite before you refactor. This is the first genuinely new use
   of mutation testing since you learned it: not grading your own
   tests, but deciding how far to trust a stranger's.
5. **Refactor or add behavior — never both in one commit.** A
   behavior-preserving commit can be reviewed by diffing structure;
   a mixed commit can only be reviewed by re-understanding
   everything, and your reviewer is drowning already. This is also
   what makes the boy-scout rule (§4.4) safe: the cleanup commit
   reverts independently of the feature commit.

### 6.3 Worked micro-example (board sketch)

The inherited `report()` is 150 lines: reads a CSV, computes three
aggregates, prints HTML. There are no tests. The month-one path:
golden-master its HTML output on five input files (one afternoon);
extract the CSV-reading into a function that takes a filename —
suite still green; extract each aggregate as a pure function — green,
and *now* the aggregates can be unit-tested directly, so write those
tests; mutation-test; only then change the aggregate marketing asked
you to change, in its own commit, with its own test arriving first.
Total new behavior: one commit. Everything before it: insurance that
the one commit touches only what it claims.

## 7. Proj3 launch: the handover, and your first week

### 7.1 Handover mechanics, tonight

Repos are exchanged **as-is**. No cleanup commits after the deadline
— the rubric watches history, and a flurry of day-after polishing
says more than it fixes. You hand over, and receive: the repo, its
issue tracker, its CI state, and a short handover note. A good
handover note says: how to run it, how to test it, the three things
most likely to bite, and the one thing the team never got working.
Honest beats flattering — **failures are findings** applies to
handovers too, and the inheriting team will discover the truth by
Thursday anyway.

### 7.2 First-week triage of the inherited repo, in order

1. **Run it.** Before reading a line. Does it build, start, and do
   its demo? The distance between the README's claims and what
   happens is your first measurement.
2. **Run the tests, and coverage.** Three numbers for your triage
   report: tests passing, suite runtime, coverage. (The course bar
   from [the patterns recap](w6.md): a coverage number must exist;
   >50% is the ask.) Then one mutation run on the core package —
   number four: how much of that green is real.
3. **Read history, not just code.** Churn map, bug-locality
   hotspots, did tests arrive with features or in a final-week
   binge? Repo health indicators (commits, PRs, issues,
   contributors over time) tell you which kind of team you
   inherited from — and they are the same indicators the proj3
   rubric watches on *you*.
4. **Pass through the plumbing once.** Make one tiny, safe change —
   fix a typo, delete a dead knob — and walk it through the full
   path: branch, test locally, PR, review, CI, merge. Day one. You
   are not testing the change; you are testing that the pipeline
   works with your names on it, before anything urgent needs it.
5. **Regenerate the LLM wiki** ([survival kit](w1.md)): point the
   model at the repo and have it build the map — architecture,
   entry points, data flow, "what should a new maintainer read
   first." Raw sources stay immutable; the model owns the wiki.
6. **Audit the wiki.** Every claim checked against the repo before
   you rely on it — the librarian fetches, you judge. Each false
   claim you catch gets *recorded, not just fixed*: it marks a spot
   where the code confuses even a careful reader, which is where
   you will be confused in week three — and caught LLM errors are
   D5 material, this course's favorite deliverable.
7. **Write the debt ledger.** One page: the knob count and the dead
   knobs (§4.5), the SATD count and the three scariest confessions
   (§4.3), the untested load-bearing functions, the duplicated
   truths, the dependency risks, where the repo sits on the
   modernization quadrant (§5.2) — and the three repairs with the
   best interest-to-principal ratio. That page is your proj3 plan's
   spine, and it is M0 thinking: claims with instruments, not
   vibes.

### 7.3 The standing warning

From [the architecture lecture's AI half-hour](w4.md): the model
that quietly rewrote a finished, relied-upon kernel went unnoticed
for days because its owner was busy on tasks two through five. You
are about to run agents over code you *barely know* — you cannot
eyeball what changed, because you do not yet know what it looked
like before. Your defense is everything above: characterization
fences, mutation-audited suites, one-step commits, verdicts on every
agent run, and worktrees so parallel agents cannot stomp each other
([git week](w2.md)).

## 8. Research corner (read at home)

- **Does debt measurably hurt?** Mining studies across hundreds of
  repos associate the classic smells (God classes, duplication,
  high churn) with higher defect rates and slower change — with the
  usual correlation/causation caveats: bad code and bad process
  share parents. The strongest result in the family you already
  know: process features (churn, history, who-touched-it) predict
  defects about as well as anything measured from the code itself —
  the Google three-feature test-ordering story is one instance of a
  general law: **in software, history is signal**.
- **SATD mining** is a live subfield: detecting debt comments,
  tracking their lifetimes, watching whether they are ever repaid
  (mostly: no). Your inherited repo is a one-repo replication
  study, and your debt ledger is its paper.
- **The configuration abyss** research program (Apel and others):
  sampling strategies for untestably-large config spaces,
  performance-influence models (which knobs actually move the
  needle), and — the Xu et al. answer — not navigating the abyss
  but *shrinking* it. Note the rhyme with this course's compact-AI
  theme: when the space is too big to search, the engineering move
  is to make it smaller.
