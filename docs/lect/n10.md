# N10: Maintenance — Living With Other People's Code (Oct 21)

Tonight proj2 dies and proj3 is born: you inherit another team's
proj2, and another team inherits yours. Everything in this lecture is
about the month you are about to have. The exam-night proj3 preview
(Oct 14) gave you the economics — most of the life of software is
maintenance, and maintainers spend most of *their* life just reading. Tonight is the practice: how to understand code you did
not write, how to see the debt hiding in it, how to change it without
breaking it, and exactly what to do in your first week with the
inherited repo.

Rough shape of the 90 minutes: the COBOL story; why comprehension is
the bottleneck; technical debt, with configuration as the worked
example; migration and modernization, with lessons from people who did
it for real; refactoring under test; then the proj3 handover
mechanics.

---

## 1. The COBOL story: the language that refused to die

Start with the numbers, because they are absurd.

- Estimates run to **hundreds of billions of lines of COBOL** still in
  production — supporting around **$3 trillion in daily commerce**.
- When a credit or debit card is swiped, roughly **95% of the time a
  COBOL program runs** somewhere in the background. Around 43% of US
  core banking systems are COBOL.
- The language shipped in **1959**. It was designed as a stop-gap.
- The average COBOL programmer is somewhere between 45 and 55 years
  old, and scarce enough that salaries carry a visible premium.

So the obvious move is: replace it. People have tried.

- **Commonwealth Bank of Australia** replaced its core banking
  platform: five years, over AU$1 billion — about double the budget,
  two years late.
- **TSB Bank, 2018**: migrated a billion customer records onto a new
  system. Customers locked out for weeks; some saw *other people's
  accounts*; roughly £330M in migration costs plus £49M lost to fraud
  during the meltdown.
- **New York Times** rewrote its COBOL circulation system in Java —
  successfully — and still blew the schedule, because the hard part
  was proving the new system replicated *every* behavior of the old
  one.

And when you do not replace it, it bites anyway. In 2020, COVID
unemployment claims surged 1,600% in New Jersey and the governor went
on television asking for volunteer COBOL programmers. A dozen states
were in the same hole. Congress shaped the CARES Act around a flat
$600 boost partly because states said changing a *percentage* in
their unemployment systems would take five months. Meanwhile a
75-year-old Texan runs a company called COBOL Cowboys ("Not our first
rodeo") renting out 350 retired engineers for exactly these
emergencies. "Some of the software I wrote for banks in the 1970s is
still being used."

Three lessons, and they are the lecture:

1. **Code outlives everything** — its authors, its language's
   fashion, sometimes its company. Software is the only engineering
   artifact we routinely run for 50 years while pretending it is
   temporary.
2. **Replacement is not the default answer.** The code is not the
   expensive part; the *behavior* is. The NYT project was hard
   because the old system's behavior was the real spec, and nobody
   had written it down. (You met this idea in testing: the oracle
   problem, at the scale of a whole system.)
3. **The maintenance bill is mostly a comprehension bill.** Nobody is
   paying COBOL Cowboys to type; they are paying them to *understand*.

One more, for this course's Q1: COBOL systems were not vibe-coded.
They came from the most planning-heavy era in software history, with
tests and operations manuals — and they are *still* this expensive to
maintain. Now ask what the maintenance bill looks like for code that
was generated fast, reviewed lightly, and never really understood by
anyone. You will find out personally, starting tonight.

## 2. Comprehension at scale

Why is understanding the bottleneck?

**Knowledge decays through handoffs.** From the
[patterns recap](w6.md): each handoff of a codebase roughly halves
the understanding — 100% in the author's head, 50% in the first
maintainer's, 25% in the next. Proj3 is handoff one. The team that
wrote your inherited repo knew why that weird branch exists; you will
not, and they will not remember by November.

**Lehman's laws (1970s, still true).** Two matter here. *Continuing
change*: a system that is used must change, because its environment
changes. *Increasing complexity*: as it changes, its structure decays
unless someone spends effort specifically to stop that. Maintenance is
not keeping the system the same; it is running to stay in place.

**Your own code counts as other people's code.** Black-box testing's
dirty secret from [the patterns lecture](n06.md): "your own code
three months later is always incomprehensible." Three months is
roughly the gap between your proj2 kickoff and proj3's end.

**Reading strategies that work** (you have met all three):

- **The librarian pattern** ([survival kit week](w1.md)): use the LLM
  to *fetch* — where is the entry point, where does the data live,
  what reads this file — then read that yourself. The librarian
  fetches; you judge.
- **Bug locality** ([patterns recap](w6.md)): bugs are social
  animals. `git log` tells you where the fires were; that is where
  comprehension effort pays first.
- **Tests as documentation**: the suite is the one description of the
  system that cannot drift without someone noticing. Read the tests
  before the code. If there are no tests — that fact is your first
  finding, and section 5 tells you what to do about it.

## 3. Technical debt, properly

Ward Cunningham's metaphor, used precisely. Debt is not "bad code."
Debt is a *loan*: you shipped sooner by deferring work, and you now
pay **interest** — every change costs extra because the deferred work
is still missing — until you repay the **principal** by doing that
work. Two honest axes: did you take the loan *deliberately or
accidentally*, and *prudently or recklessly*. "We'll ship without
docs and write them next sprint" is deliberate-prudent.
"What's a design?" is reckless-accidental, and it is also what an
unwatched code generator produces at scale.

The point of the metaphor is management, not guilt: debt you know
about, with a plan, is a tool. Debt nobody wrote down is just rot
with a nicer name. Your proj3 first deliverable is, in effect, an
audit of the previous team's unrecorded loans.

### 3.1 The worked example: configuration debt

The sharpest measured case of debt nobody meant to take. Xu et al.,
["Hey, you have given me too many knobs!"](https://doi.org/10.1145/2786805.2786852)
(FSE 2015) studied a commercial storage system plus Apache, MySQL and
Hadoop:

<img src="https://raw.githubusercontent.com/txt/se26f/refs/heads/main/etc/img/knobs-growth.png" width="420" align=right>

- Parameter counts **only ever grow** (figure at right): parameters
  are added ~7× faster than they are removed. Hadoop MapReduce went
  from 17 to 173 in seven years; MySQL 5.6 had 461.
- **Up to 54% of parameters are set by almost nobody**; only 6–17%
  are set by a majority of users.
- The cost is real: up to 48.5% of user configuration issues are
  people struggling to *find or set* the right knob, and roughly half
  of configuration errors come from users keeping a default they
  should have changed.
- Their guidelines could remove **51.9%** of the storage system's
  parameters with little impact on real users.

<img src="https://raw.githubusercontent.com/txt/se26f/refs/heads/main/etc/img/knobs-configurable.png" width="420" align=right>

And here is the moment the debt is born (figure at right): a real
developer discussion from HDFS. *"Since we are not sure what is a
good choice, how about making it configurable?"* Every knob like this
is a decision the developer declined to make, exported to thousands
of users — each one a line of documentation, a test matrix dimension,
a support-ticket generator, and attack surface. It is the Word-ribbon
finding from [the patterns lecture](n06.md) (90% of use in 10 of 60
icons), measured on the inside of the program. It is also the Google
feature-toggle spiderweb from
[the architecture lecture](n04.md) wearing different clothes. Sven
Apel calls the research version of this the *configuration abyss*
(interview in IEEE Software, 2024): the config space is so large that
nobody — not the devs, not the users — knows which corner of it they
are actually running.

Rob Pike, quoted in the paper: "There is too much configuration.
There are too many options. Stuff should just work."

**Proj3 translation.** Count your inherited repo's knobs: config
files, environment variables, CLI flags, magic constants promoted to
"settings." For each: who sets this, ever? A knob nobody sets is debt
you can retire in week one — and retiring it is a *measurable*
maintenance contribution (and remember the single-source-of-truth
rule when you do: one home per remaining setting).

## 4. Migration and modernization: lessons from people who did it

So the inherited system is debt-heavy. Rewrite it? Almost never —
section 1 priced that. The field's actual menu is richer. Wesley
Assunção (NC State, down the hall — his group studies exactly this)
frames it as portfolio analysis:

<img src="https://raw.githubusercontent.com/txt/se26f/refs/heads/main/etc/img/modernize-quadrant.png" width="430" align=right>

**Five options by business value × technical quality** (figure at
right, from
[Contemporary Software Modernization](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.04017)):
low value, low quality → **replace** with something off the shelf;
low value, high quality → just **maintain**; high value, high quality
→ **evolve** (new features on good bones); high value, low quality →
**re-engineer** (keep the business rules, pay the debt); and when new
technology is the point → **migrate**. Note what this grid does: it
forbids the lazy question "is this code good?" and forces the paired
question "good *and worth what*?" Your inherited proj2 sits somewhere
on this grid, and your proj3 plan should say where and why.

Lessons from the industrial migrations his group reports (a
large-scale migration to microservices, SANER 2026; and using LLMs to
re-engineer a legacy system at Volvo, SANER 2025):

1. **Never big-bang.** Working migrations run old and new side by
   side and move one slice at a time — the *strangler fig* pattern:
   new growth wraps the old tree, takes over a branch at a time, and
   the old trunk dies only at the end. You met the testing half of
   this already: **shadow testing** ([deeper testing week](w5.md)) —
   the old system serves the user while the new one runs silently
   beside it, and you diff.
2. **The old system's behavior is the spec** — including its bugs,
   which downstream systems may depend on. Budget for discovering
   requirements, not just porting code. (Reuse means *more* testing,
   not less — the NASA lesson from [requirements week](w3.md).)
3. **LLMs help at the comprehension end** — summarizing, mapping,
   drafting the port — and every output needs an audit, because a
   plausible wrong summary of legacy code is worse than no summary.
   Volvo's experience, and also exactly your proj3 wiki task below.
4. **Organizations migrate, not just code.** Who owns the seam while
   half the system is old and half new? If nobody, that is the
   interface-gap failure (Mars Climate Orbiter) from
   [requirements week](w3.md), scheduled months in advance.

<img src="https://raw.githubusercontent.com/txt/se26f/refs/heads/main/etc/img/modernize-workflow.png" width="90%">

Their workflow sketch (above) is initiation → planning → execution →
transition, with arrows pointing *backwards* — you will return to
planning mid-execution, on purpose. Proj3 is this diagram at course
scale: tonight is initiation; your first-week triage is planning.

## 5. Refactoring under test

Refactoring: changing structure while preserving behavior. The
definition hides a trap — "preserving behavior" is a *claim*, and
claims need instruments. So the iron rule:

> **No green suite, no refactoring.** A refactor without tests is
> just editing with confidence.

But the inherited repo may be thin on tests. Michael Feathers'
definition: *legacy code is code without tests* — regardless of age.
His escape route, which is your proj3 playbook:

1. **Characterization tests first.** Before changing anything, write
   tests that pin down what the code *currently does* — not what it
   should do. Run the function, observe the answer, assert that
   answer. You are not approving the behavior; you are fencing it, so
   change becomes detectable. (This is the plan↔test loop run
   backwards: reverse-engineering expectations from an artifact —
   the same move as the vibe-coding recovery in
   [week one](w1.md).)
2. **Small steps, test between each.** Rename; run. Extract a
   function; run. The one-line-of-code stories from
   [week one](w1.md) are the reason: one line is enough to matter,
   so one step is the right batch size.
3. **Audit the net you are trusting.** A green suite that asserts
   nothing is a fence made of fog — the order-controller story from
   [the patterns recap](w6.md) passed for a whole semester *because*
   of its bug. Mutation-test the inherited suite before you lean on
   it: break the code on purpose; if nothing goes red, extend the
   tests before you refactor.
4. **Refactor or add behavior — never both in one commit.** A
   behavior-preserving commit can be reviewed by diffing structure;
   a mixed commit can only be reviewed by re-understanding
   everything. (And your reviewer is drowning already; see the
   review shorthand — SSOT?, SoC? — they will be sending you.)

## 6. Proj3 launch: the handover, and your first week

Mechanics tonight: repos are exchanged as-is. No cleanup commits
after the deadline — the rubric watches history, and a flurry of
day-after polishing says more than it fixes.

**First-week triage of the inherited repo.** In order:

1. **Run it.** Before reading a line. Does it build, start, and do
   its demo? The distance between the README's claims and what
   happens is your first measurement.
2. **Run the tests, and coverage.** Three numbers in your triage
   report: tests passing, runtime of the suite, coverage. (The
   course bar from [the patterns recap](w6.md): a coverage number
   must exist; >50% is the ask.)
3. **Read history, not just code.** Where were the fires
   (bug-locality), which files churn, did tests arrive with features
   or in a final-week heroic binge? Repo health indicators tell you
   which kind of team you inherited from.
4. **Regenerate the LLM wiki** ([survival kit week](w1.md)): point
   the model at the repo and have it build the map — architecture,
   entry points, data flow, "what would you read first." Raw sources
   stay immutable; the model owns the wiki.
5. **Audit the wiki.** Every claim gets checked against the repo
   before you rely on it — the librarian fetches, you judge. Each
   false claim you catch is worth recording: it marks a place where
   the code confuses even a careful reader, which is where *you*
   will be confused in week three. (It is also D5 material: caught
   LLM errors are the deliverable.)
6. **Write the debt ledger.** One page: the knob count (§3.1), the
   untested load-bearing functions, the duplicated truths, where the
   repo sits on the modernization quadrant (§4), and the three
   repairs with the best interest-to-principal ratio. That page is
   your proj3 plan's spine — and it is M0 thinking: claims with
   instruments, not vibes.

And a warning from this course's own ledger
([the architecture lecture's AI half-hour](w4.md)): the model that
quietly rewrote a finished, relied-upon kernel went unnoticed for
days because its owner was busy on tasks two through five.
You are about to run agents over code you *barely know* — which
means you cannot eyeball what changed. Your defense is everything
above: characterization tests, mutation-audited suites, small
commits, verdicts on every agent run.

## 7. Review questions

Exam-style: **(a)** recall, **(b)** analysis or transfer.

**1. COBOL economics.**
(a) Give two of the numbers that explain why COBOL is still running,
and name the 2018 bank migration that showed customers other people's
accounts.
(b) "The code is not the expensive part; the behavior is." Use the
New York Times rewrite to explain that sentence, then say which
testing technique from this course directly addresses it.

**2. Debt.**
(a) Define technical debt using interest and principal, and name the
two axes of the honest-debt quadrant.
(b) Your inherited repo has 38 configuration options; you can find
users for six. Using the FSE'15 findings, argue for retiring most of
the rest, state the cost of keeping them, and say which ones you must
keep anyway.

**3. Knobs.**
(a) Why did the HDFS developers in Figure 2 add the parameter, and
how many users did the researchers find for it?
(b) "Since we are not sure what is a good choice, how about making it
configurable?" Rewrite that sentence as what it really is — a
deferred decision — and say who pays for the deferral, and when.

**4. Modernization.**
(a) Name the five options on the modernization quadrant and the two
axes that pick between them.
(b) Place your actual inherited proj2 on the quadrant. Justify the
placement with one measurement from your triage, and state what the
placement forbids you from doing this month.

**5. Refactoring under test.**
(a) What is a characterization test, and what is Feathers' definition
of legacy code?
(b) Your inherited suite is green with 70% coverage. Describe the
check you run before trusting it as a refactoring net, what a bad
result looks like, and your next move if the result is bad.

**6. The first week.**
(a) List the six first-week triage steps in order.
(b) Your regenerated LLM wiki claims "all database access goes
through `store.py`." Describe how you audit that one claim, what you
record if it is false, and why a false wiki claim is still useful.
